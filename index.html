Cardiovascular and Cerebrovascular

16 parameters
Blood Viscosity(N)
This measures how thick and syrupy your blood is. Blood needs to flow smoothly through your vessels — if it becomes too thick, it moves more slowly and puts extra strain on your heart. People with high blood viscosity are at greater risk of strokes, heart attacks, and blood clots. Dehydration, high cholesterol, and certain conditions can all increase blood viscosity.
Hyperviscosity state
When blood viscosity is too high, blood flows sluggishly through the vessels. For people with high blood pressure or heart disease, this significantly raises the risk of a stroke or heart attack. Think of it like trying to pump honey through a pipe instead of water — the heart has to work much harder.
Cholesterol Crystal
Cholesterol crystals form when there is too much cholesterol in the blood and it starts to clump. High levels are associated with early-stage artery hardening (atherosclerosis) and chest pain. Low levels may indicate poor nutrition or a weakened immune system. Both extremes are worth monitoring.
Blood Fat
Blood fats (lipids) include cholesterol and triglycerides. When these are too high, fatty deposits build up inside artery walls, narrowing them over time. This is a major risk factor for heart disease and stroke. High blood fats can be caused by genetics, an unhealthy diet, diabetes, thyroid problems, or kidney disease.
Vascular Resistance
This measures how hard your heart has to push to move blood through your blood vessels. Higher resistance means narrower or stiffer vessels — a sign of high blood pressure or arterial disease. Lower resistance means blood flows easily. When resistance is too low, it can indicate abnormally low blood pressure.
Vascular Elasticity
Healthy arteries are flexible — they expand when the heart beats and spring back between beats. This elasticity acts as a natural buffer, smoothing out blood flow. Stiff, inelastic arteries (a sign of aging or arterial disease) mean less buffering, higher peak blood pressure, and increased strain on the heart.
Myocardial Blood Demand
This is the amount of blood the heart muscle needs to receive every minute to function properly. Like any working muscle, the heart demands more blood when it is working harder. If the coronary arteries cannot meet this demand, it can cause chest pain (angina) or a heart attack.
Myocardial Blood Perfusion Volume
This is the actual amount of blood being delivered to the heart muscle per minute. If this falls below what the heart needs (the blood demand), the heart muscle becomes starved of oxygen — a condition that can cause chest pain, damage the heart muscle, or trigger a heart attack.
Myocardial Oxygen Consumption
This is the amount of oxygen the heart muscle uses per minute. The heart's oxygen use goes up when your heart beats faster, contracts more forcefully, or beats for longer. Ideally, the heart should do maximum useful work while consuming as little oxygen as possible — this is the sign of an efficient, healthy heart.
Stroke Volume
The amount of blood your heart pumps out with each single beat. A healthy resting adult pumps around 60–100 mL per beat. Low stroke volume means the heart is not filling or contracting efficiently. This can be caused by blood loss, dehydration, heart muscle weakness, or stiff arteries making it harder to eject blood.
Left Ventricular Ejection Impedance
This measures the resistance the left ventricle (the main pumping chamber) faces when it tries to push blood out into the aorta. High impedance — caused by things like a narrowed aortic valve or high blood pressure — means the heart must squeeze much harder to eject blood, which strains and thickens the heart muscle over time.
Left Ventricular Effective Pump Power
This reflects how powerfully the left ventricle contracts to push blood around the body. The normal resting value is about 1.8 kg of force. Low pump power means the heart is contracting weakly — the heart muscle fibres may be damaged or diseased. High pump power means the heart is contracting strongly and ejecting a good volume of blood.
Coronary Artery Elasticity
The coronary arteries are the three blood vessels that supply the heart muscle itself with blood. Just like other arteries, they need to remain flexible and open to deliver adequate blood flow. When they stiffen or narrow — due to plaque buildup from high cholesterol, smoking, diabetes, or high blood pressure — the heart muscle is at risk of oxygen starvation.
Coronary Perfusion Pressure
This is the blood pressure that drives blood through the coronary arteries to nourish the heart muscle. It is mainly determined by the blood pressure during the relaxation phase of the heartbeat (diastolic pressure). When coronary perfusion pressure is too low, parts of the heart muscle do not receive enough blood, which can progress to a heart attack.
Cerebral Blood Vessel Elasticity
This reflects the flexibility and health of the blood vessels supplying the brain. When brain arteries become stiff and narrow — due to atherosclerosis (hardening of the arteries) — blood flow to the brain is reduced. This raises the risk of blood clots forming in the brain (cerebral thrombosis) or blood vessels bursting (haemorrhagic stroke).
Brain Tissue Blood Supply Status
This measures how well blood is reaching the brain tissue. The brain depends entirely on continuous blood flow for oxygen and nutrients. When blood supply is disrupted — either because a vessel is blocked (ischaemic) or because a vessel bursts (haemorrhagic) — brain cells begin to die within minutes, causing a stroke.

Gastrointestinal Function

5 parameters
Pepsin Secretion Coefficient
This measures how well your stomach is producing pepsin, the enzyme that breaks down proteins from your food. The stomach also produces hydrochloric acid and protective mucus. Together, these help digest food and kill harmful bacteria. Too little pepsin means protein digestion is impaired; too much can contribute to stomach ulcers if the protective mucus lining is weakened.
Gastric Peristalsis Function Coefficient
Your stomach walls are made of layers of muscle that squeeze and churn your food — a movement called peristalsis. This mixing action combines food with digestive juices to break it down into a smooth paste (chyme) that can pass into the small intestine. Poor peristalsis means food sits in the stomach too long, causing bloating, discomfort, and poor digestion.
Gastric Absorption Function Coefficient
While the stomach is mainly a digestion organ, its lining does absorb some substances — primarily water, alcohol, and certain medications. An adult stomach produces 1.5–2.5 litres of gastric juice per day. This index measures how effectively the stomach's lining is functioning in its absorption and protective roles.
Small Intestine Peristalsis Function Coefficient
The small intestine moves food along using rhythmic contractions (peristalsis). This movement mixes food with digestive enzymes, pushes it against the intestinal wall for absorption, and propels it towards the large intestine. Slow peristalsis leads to bloating, constipation, and poor nutrient absorption; too fast means nutrients are not fully absorbed.
Small Intestine Absorption Function Coefficient
This is where almost all your nutrients are absorbed. The small intestine absorbs sugars (broken into simple glucose), amino acids (from proteins), and fats (broken into fatty acids). It also absorbs vitamins, minerals, and water. Poor absorption here leads to nutritional deficiencies, weight loss, and digestive symptoms even if you are eating enough.

Large Intestine Function

4 parameters
Large intestine peristalsis function coefficient
The large intestine (colon) moves waste material along slowly — much more slowly than the small intestine — giving it time to absorb water and electrolytes. If the colon moves too slowly, too much water is removed from waste, causing hard stools and constipation. If it moves too fast, not enough water is absorbed, causing loose stools and diarrhoea.
Colonic absorption coefficient
The colon's primary job is to absorb water and minerals (electrolytes) from waste material, converting liquid waste into solid faeces. It can absorb up to 1.5 litres of water per day. Good colonic absorption helps maintain the body's fluid and electrolyte balance. Poor absorption leads to dehydration or electrolyte imbalances.
Intestinal bacteria coefficient
A healthy gut contains billions of beneficial bacteria that create a slightly acidic environment, crowd out harmful bacteria, aid digestion, and support the immune system. This index measures the balance between good and bad bacteria in your gut (the microbiome). An imbalance — caused by antibiotics, poor diet, or illness — is linked to digestive problems, weakened immunity, and a range of other health issues.
Intraluminal pressure coefficient
This measures the gas pressure inside the intestine. Some gas is normal — it is produced by bacteria fermenting food. Excessive gas causes bloating, cramps, and discomfort. Too much gas pressure can result from slow intestinal movement, food intolerances, swallowing air, or an imbalance in gut bacteria.

Liver Function

5 parameters
Protein Metabolism
The liver is your body's protein factory. It takes amino acids absorbed from digested food and builds them into hundreds of different proteins your body needs — including blood proteins like albumin, clotting factors, and immune proteins. It also breaks down excess or damaged proteins. Poor liver protein metabolism leads to fluid retention, clotting problems, and weakened immunity.
Energy Production Function
The liver is the body's central energy processing plant. It converts digested carbohydrates into glucose for immediate energy use, stores excess glucose as glycogen for later, and converts fat into energy when needed. When you haven't eaten for a while, the liver releases stored glucose to keep your blood sugar stable.
Detoxification Function
The liver neutralises harmful substances — including alcohol, drugs, metabolic waste, and environmental toxins — and converts them into harmless forms that can be excreted in urine or bile. This is one of the liver's most critical functions. A damaged liver cannot detoxify properly, allowing harmful substances to build up in the body.
Bile Secretion Function
The liver produces bile, a digestive fluid that is stored in the gallbladder and released into the small intestine after meals. Bile breaks down dietary fats so they can be absorbed. It also carries fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K) into the body. Without adequate bile, fat digestion and absorption of these essential vitamins are impaired.
Liver Fat Content
The liver normally contains a small amount of fat. A fatty liver (hepatic steatosis) is diagnosed when fat makes up more than 5% of the liver's weight. This is commonly caused by excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, or diabetes (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease). If untreated, a fatty liver can progress to inflammation, scarring (cirrhosis), and liver failure.

Gallbladder Function

5 parameters
Serum Globulin (A/G)
This measures the ratio of albumin to globulin proteins in your blood. Albumin helps carry nutrients and maintain fluid balance, while globulins are your immune proteins. A healthy ratio means your liver is producing the right mix of proteins. A low ratio can indicate liver disease, kidney problems, or immune system issues.
Total Bilirubin (TBIL)
Bilirubin is a yellow pigment made when your body breaks down old red blood cells. The liver processes it and sends it into bile. High bilirubin causes jaundice (yellowing of skin and eyes) and may signal liver problems, gallstones blocking the bile duct, or excessive red blood cell breakdown.
Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)
An enzyme found mainly in your liver, bones, and bile ducts. High levels often indicate a bile duct blockage (such as from gallstones), liver disease, or rapid bone growth. It is routinely checked to assess liver and gallbladder health.
Serum Total Bile Acid (TBA)
Bile acids are made in the liver and stored in the gallbladder to help digest fats. Measuring total bile acids in the blood is one of the most sensitive ways to detect early liver disease. Elevated levels suggest the liver is not processing bile correctly.
Bilirubin (DBIL)
Direct bilirubin is the processed form that the liver has made water-soluble. High levels indicate the liver has processed bilirubin, but it cannot flow properly into the intestines — often caused by bile duct blockage, gallstones, or liver cell damage.

Pancreatic Function

3 parameters
Insulin
Insulin is a hormone produced by the pancreas that acts like a key, unlocking your body's cells to let glucose (sugar) in from the bloodstream for energy. Without enough insulin — or if your cells stop responding to it — blood sugar rises too high, damaging blood vessels and organs over time. This is the mechanism behind diabetes.
Pancreatic Polypeptide
Pancreatic polypeptide is a hormone released by the pancreas mainly in response to eating. It helps regulate digestion by slowing down the release of digestive enzymes and the movement of the gut. It also plays a role in appetite regulation and energy balance, though its exact functions in humans are still being studied.
Glucagon
Glucagon is the hormone that does the opposite of insulin. When blood sugar drops too low, the pancreas releases glucagon to signal the liver to release stored glucose back into the bloodstream. Insulin and glucagon work as a pair to keep blood sugar within a healthy range — like a thermostat for blood glucose levels.

Kidney Function

4 parameters
Urobilinogen Index
Urobilinogen is a colourless chemical formed in the intestines when bacteria break down bilirubin (a waste product from old red blood cells). Some is reabsorbed into the blood and excreted by the kidneys into urine. Elevated urobilinogen in urine can indicate liver disease or increased red blood cell breakdown. Very low levels may indicate bile duct obstruction.
Uric acid Index
Uric acid is a waste product formed when the body breaks down purines (found in meat, organ meats, and some seafood). Normally it dissolves in the blood and is excreted by the kidneys. High uric acid levels cause it to crystallise in joints, leading to the intensely painful condition called gout. It can also form kidney stones.
Blood urea nitrogen(BUN) Index
Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) measures a waste product from protein breakdown. The liver converts the by-products of protein digestion into urea, which the kidneys then filter out of the blood into urine. High BUN suggests the kidneys are not filtering efficiently — possibly due to kidney disease, dehydration, or high protein intake. Low BUN may indicate liver problems or malnutrition.
Proteinuria Index
Healthy kidneys filter waste while keeping proteins in the blood. If protein appears in the urine in significant amounts (proteinuria), it means the kidney filters are damaged and leaking. Proteinuria is an early warning sign of kidney disease, diabetes-related kidney damage, or high blood pressure affecting the kidneys.

Lung Function

5 parameters
Vital Capacity (VC)
The maximum amount of air you can breathe out after taking the deepest breath possible. It reflects the overall size and strength of your lungs. Lower values may indicate asthma, COPD, or weakened breathing muscles.
Total Lung Capacity (TLC)
The total volume of air your lungs can hold when fully inflated. It helps doctors understand whether your lungs are restricted (too small) or overinflated (as in emphysema). Normal values depend on your age, height, and sex.
Airway Resistance (RAM)
How easily air flows through your airways. Higher resistance means your airways are narrower or partially blocked — as seen in asthma or bronchitis — making breathing harder. Lower resistance means air moves freely.
Arterial Oxygen Content (PaO2)
How well your lungs are transferring oxygen into your bloodstream. Low oxygen levels (hypoxemia) cause shortness of breath, confusion, and organ damage if untreated.
Arterial Carbon Dioxide (PaCO2)
The pressure of carbon dioxide in your arterial blood — the waste gas you exhale. High levels mean you are not breathing enough to remove CO2. Low levels may mean you are breathing too fast (hyperventilation).

Brain Nerve

6 parameters
Status of Brain Tissue Blood Supply
This measures how well blood is circulating through the tiny vessels of the brain. The brain uses about 20% of your body's total blood supply despite being only 2% of your body weight. Poor blood supply to the brain — even in tiny vessels — can cause memory problems, headaches, dizziness, and over time increases the risk of stroke and dementia.
Cerebral Arteriosclerosis
Cerebral arteriosclerosis means the arteries supplying your brain have become stiff and narrowed — usually due to fatty plaques building up inside the artery walls. This restricts blood flow to the brain and increases the risk of blood clots forming. Symptoms can include dizziness, headaches, memory decline, and in severe cases, a stroke (a blockage cutting off blood to part of the brain).
Functional Status of Cranial Nerve
Your cranial nerves are 12 pairs of nerves that connect directly to the brain — controlling things like vision, hearing, smell, taste, facial movement, swallowing, and the gag reflex. This index reflects how well these nerves are functioning. Damage or disease affecting the cranial nerves can impair senses and muscle control in the head and neck.
The communication among the nerve cells of the three parts depends on two factors
Brain cells communicate through vast networks of connections. You are born with about 100 billion brain cells, but what matters more than the number of cells is the number and quality of connections between them. The more connections you have — built through learning, mental stimulation, and experience — the better your brain functions.
Sentiment Index
This reflects your emotional wellbeing — specifically whether your emotional responses to life events are positive or negative. Positive emotions (happiness, contentment, hope) strengthen the immune system and promote overall health. Persistent negative emotions (chronic stress, anxiety, sadness) weaken immunity, raise blood pressure, and increase the risk of physical illness.
Memory Index(ZS)
This reflects the strength and reliability of your memory. Memory decline can result from reduced blood flow to the brain, brain cell death (atrophy), or conditions like Alzheimer's disease. Memory is broadly split into short-term memory (what you just experienced) and long-term memory (what you have learned over your lifetime). Maintaining good brain circulation and mental activity helps preserve memory.

Bone Disease

4 parameters
Lumbar Fiber Protruding Dimension
This measures how much the discs between your lower back (lumbar) vertebrae are bulging or herniating outward. When a spinal disc slips or bulges, it can press on nearby nerves, causing back pain, leg pain (sciatica), numbness, or weakness. The greater the protrusion, the more pressure is placed on the nerve root.
Adhesion Degree of Shoulder Muscle
This measures how stiff or 'stuck' the shoulder muscles are — a sign of inflammation or frozen shoulder. When the tissues around the shoulder joint tighten and scar, movement becomes painful and restricted. The lower the score, the healthier and more mobile the shoulder is.
Limbs Circulation Limit
This measures how restricted blood circulation is in your arms and legs. Poor microcirculation in the limbs — caused by cold, inactivity, artery disease, or diabetes — leads to numbness, coldness, and cramps. A lower score indicates better circulation; four pluses indicates the most severe restriction.
Age of Ligament
This is a composite score that reflects the overall 'biological age' of your joints and ligaments based on the other bone disease indicators. A higher percentage means greater signs of wear, aging, or degeneration in your musculoskeletal system. A lower percentage means your joints are in good condition relative to your age.

Bone Mineral Density

6 parameters
Osteoclast Coefficient
Osteoclasts are large cells that break down old bone tissue as part of the natural bone renewal process. Healthy bone constantly renews itself — osteoclasts remove old bone and osteoblasts build new bone. When osteoclast activity becomes too high without matching bone-building activity, bones lose density and become fragile (osteoporosis).
Amount of Calcium Loss
This measures how much calcium your bones are losing. While calcium is essential for bone strength, modern research shows that bone loss is not simply about getting too little calcium — it also involves vitamin D levels, mechanical stress (exercise), hormones, and other minerals. Significant calcium loss weakens bones and increases fracture risk.

Vitamin D, exercise (particularly weight-bearing activities), hormones, and trace minerals like strontium and boron are all essential factors in maintaining bone density. A comprehensive approach is more effective than focusing on calcium alone.
Degree of Bone Hyperplasia
Bone hyperplasia (also called bone spurs or osteophytes) is when the body grows extra bone in response to stress, injury, or wear. It is a sign that normal bone structure has changed. Common sites include the knee ('bone-on-bone' pain), spine, and heels. While the extra bone itself is not necessarily painful, it can press on nerves or restrict movement.
Degree of Osteoporosis
Osteoporosis means 'porous bones' — bones that have lost density and become fragile. While the mineral content of bones remains relatively normal, the overall bone mass (including the protein scaffolding that gives bones their structure) is significantly reduced. This makes bones prone to fractures from minor falls or even just bending forward.
Bone Mineral Density
Bone mineral density (BMD) is the gold standard test for diagnosing osteoporosis. It measures how tightly packed the calcium and other minerals are in your bones. Low BMD means weaker bones and a higher fracture risk. BMD is most commonly tested in the hip and spine using a DEXA scan. Post-menopausal women, older men, and those on certain medications are most at risk of low BMD.

Rheumatoid Bone Disease

5 parameters
Degree of Cervical Calcification
This measures the extent of calcium deposits forming in the neck (cervical) vertebrae due to bone overgrowth. No calcification means the bones are normal. Basic calcification (30%+) indicates moderate bone spurs forming. Full calcification (70%+) means significant bone spur development that may be pressing on nerves, causing neck pain, stiffness, headaches, or arm numbness.
Degree of Lumbar Calcification
This measures the extent of calcium deposits in the lower back (lumbar) vertebrae from bone overgrowth. As with the cervical spine, increasing calcification indicates worsening degeneration of the spinal discs and joints. Advanced calcification can cause chronic back pain, limited flexibility, and nerve compression causing leg pain (sciatica).
Bone Hyperplasia Coefficient
This measures how much abnormal extra bone your body has grown in response to wear and damage. Bone hyperplasia (bone spurs) occurs when bones try to repair stress and injury by building extra bone. While sometimes painless, bone spurs in joints and the spine can cause pain, stiffness, and restricted movement.
Osteoporosis Coefficient
This measures the overall degree of bone thinning in your body. In osteoporosis, the protein scaffolding of bone is significantly reduced even though mineral concentration may be relatively normal. The result is fragile, honeycomb-like bone structure that is prone to fracture — especially in the hip, wrist, and spine.
Rheumatism Coefficient
Rheumatism refers to a broad group of conditions affecting joints, muscles, and connective tissue. Broadly, it includes any painful disorder of the joints, muscles, or fibrous tissue. In its more specific form, rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease where the immune system attacks joint linings, causing painful inflammation, swelling, stiffness, and long-term joint damage.

Bone Growth Index

5 parameters
Bone alkaline phosphatase
This enzyme is secreted by bone-building cells (osteoblasts) and is one of the best markers of bone formation activity. When calcium is not being properly deposited into bone — as in rickets or osteomalacia — this enzyme level rises as the bones try harder to mineralise. It is used to monitor bone diseases and assess how well bone-building treatments are working.
Osteocalcin
Osteocalcin is a protein produced exclusively by bone-building cells (osteoblasts). Its level in the blood reflects how actively new bone is being made. Higher osteocalcin means faster bone turnover — which can be normal during growth or indicate high-turnover osteoporosis (as seen after menopause). Lower levels in elderly people reflect slower bone renewal.
Status of long bone healing
Long bones are the bones in your arms and legs (such as the femur, tibia, and humerus). They have a dense shaft and spongy ends. This index reflects how well any damage or fractures to long bones are healing. Good healing requires adequate calcium, vitamin D, protein, and blood supply to the bone.
Short bone cartilage healing situation
Short bones include those in the wrist (carpal bones) and foot (tarsal bones) and are designed to absorb and spread pressure. They are covered in cartilage at joint surfaces. This index reflects the health and healing of the cartilage covering these bones. Damaged cartilage in these bones causes pain with weight-bearing and can lead to arthritis.
Epiphyseal line
The epiphyseal plate (growth plate) is a disc of cartilage near the ends of long bones in children and teenagers where new bone grows. As growth is completed, this plate becomes a solid line of bone called the epiphyseal line. In children, an open growth plate means the bone is still growing. In adults, a visible epiphyseal line confirms that growth has stopped.

Blood Sugar

3 parameters
Coefficient of Insulin Secretion
This measures how well your pancreas is producing insulin in response to rising blood sugar levels. When you eat, blood sugar rises and the pancreas releases insulin to bring it back down. If the pancreas produces too little insulin or the cells stop responding properly to it, blood sugar stays too high — the hallmark of diabetes. Consistently high blood sugar damages blood vessels, nerves, and organs over time.
Blood Sugar Coefficient
Blood sugar (blood glucose) is the main fuel your body runs on. The body tightly regulates blood sugar levels — using insulin to lower it and glucagon to raise it. When blood sugar is consistently too high (hyperglycaemia), it is a sign of diabetes or prediabetes. When it drops too low (hypoglycaemia), it can cause dizziness, confusion, and in severe cases, loss of consciousness.
Urine Sugar Coefficient
Normally, the kidneys filter glucose from the blood and reabsorb it all back — so there is no sugar in urine. When blood sugar rises above a certain threshold (about 10 mmol/L), the kidneys can no longer reabsorb it all and glucose 'spills' into the urine. Glucose in urine (glycosuria) is a classic warning sign of uncontrolled diabetes.

Trace Element

19 parameters
Calcium(Ca)
Calcium is the most abundant mineral in the body — almost all of it is stored in your bones and teeth, giving them their strength and hardness. The remaining 1% circulates in the blood and is critical for muscle contraction (including the heartbeat), nerve signalling, and blood clotting. Low calcium leads to weak bones, muscle cramps, and nerve irritability.
Iron(Fe)
Iron is essential for making haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from your lungs to every cell in your body. Iron deficiency is the world's most common nutritional deficiency, causing iron-deficiency anaemia — symptoms include fatigue, paleness, shortness of breath, and difficulty concentrating. Good sources include red meat, legumes, and leafy greens.
Zinc(Zn)
Zinc is needed to activate over 300 enzymes in your body that power everything from DNA production to immune defence to wound healing. It is also essential for normal growth, taste and smell, and male fertility. Zinc deficiency causes poor immune function, slow wound healing, loss of taste and smell, and growth problems in children.
Selenium(Se)
Selenium is a powerful antioxidant mineral that protects cells from damage caused by free radicals (unstable molecules that age the body). It supports the immune system, thyroid function, and helps the body utilise calcium properly. Deficiency weakens antioxidant defences and is associated with thyroid disorders, cardiovascular disease, and impaired immunity.
Phosphorus(P)
Phosphorus is the second most abundant mineral in the body, found mainly in bones and teeth alongside calcium. It is also essential for energy production (in the molecule ATP), cell membrane structure, and DNA. Most diets provide plenty of phosphorus — the concern is usually excess, which can interfere with calcium absorption and bone health, especially in people over 40.
Potassium(K)
Potassium is an essential electrolyte found mainly inside cells. It regulates fluid balance, muscle contraction (including the heart), and nerve signals. Low potassium (hypokalaemia) causes muscle weakness, cramps, irregular heartbeat, and fatigue. A diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and legumes provides good potassium levels. High potassium (hyperkalaemia) is dangerous, particularly for people with kidney disease.
Magnesium(Mg)
Magnesium is involved in over 300 biochemical reactions in the body. It helps control blood sugar, blood pressure, muscle and nerve function, and bone building. It also helps the body use vitamin D and calcium. Magnesium deficiency — common in people who eat little fruit and vegetables — can cause muscle cramps, irritability, insomnia, and irregular heartbeat.
Copper(Cu)
Copper works alongside iron to help the body make red blood cells. It also supports bone strength, the immune system, collagen formation, and nerve function. Copper deficiency is rare but can cause anaemia, bone fractures, poor growth, and skin and hair depigmentation. The body needs only tiny amounts — found in shellfish, nuts, seeds, and organ meats.
Cobalt(Co)
Cobalt is a component of vitamin B12, which the body cannot make without it. Vitamin B12 is essential for making red blood cells and maintaining the nervous system. Cobalt is needed in only trace amounts and is obtained entirely through dietary vitamin B12. Excessive cobalt (from industrial exposure, not diet) can damage the heart, lungs, and thyroid.
Manganese(Mn)
Manganese is needed to activate enzymes that build bone, process cholesterol, and produce energy. It also plays a role in brain function and blood clotting. Deficiency during pregnancy or childhood can impair bone growth and cause neurological problems. Manganese is found in wholegrains, nuts, seeds, and leafy vegetables.
Iodine(I)
Iodine is essential for producing thyroid hormones, which regulate metabolism, growth, and development. About 70–80% of your body's iodine is stored in the thyroid gland. Iodine deficiency is the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability worldwide. In adults, it causes an enlarged thyroid (goitre), fatigue, weight gain, and sensitivity to cold. Most people get iodine from iodised salt and seafood.
Nickel(Ni)
Nickel is present in the body in tiny amounts and appears to play a role in activating certain enzymes and supporting the function of vitamin B12. While the body needs very little nickel, it is found widely in plant foods, grains, and seaweed. Nickel deficiency is rare. However, nickel is also a common allergen — contact with nickel in jewellery or industrial environments can cause skin rashes.
Fluorine(F)
Fluorine (as fluoride) in appropriate amounts strengthens tooth enamel and bone. This is why fluoride is added to toothpaste and in some regions to drinking water. However, excessive fluoride — from contaminated water or industrial exposure — causes dental fluorosis (yellow, pitted teeth) and in severe cases skeletal fluorosis, which makes bones brittle and joints stiff.
Molybdenum(Mo)
Molybdenum is a trace mineral that activates several enzymes involved in breaking down amino acids and processing waste products. The body needs only tiny amounts — about 9 mg total in an adult — and it is found in legumes, grains, and organ meats. Deficiency is extremely rare. Molybdenum supports the liver's detoxification processes.
Vanadium(V)
Vanadium is a trace mineral that appears to play a role in bone and tooth development, blood cell production, and supporting normal blood sugar regulation (it can mimic some effects of insulin). It may help lower blood pressure and cholesterol at appropriate levels. The body needs only tiny amounts and deficiency is rare in those eating a varied diet.
Tin(Sn)
Tin is believed to be an essential trace element that supports protein and nucleic acid metabolism, contributing to normal growth and development. Deficiency in animals leads to slowed growth and hair loss. While tin's precise role in humans is not fully understood, it is present in many foods and the body maintains very low tissue levels.
Silicon(Si)
Silicon (as silica) is important for the formation of collagen and connective tissue, contributing to the strength and flexibility of skin, tendons, cartilage, and bones. It is particularly important during childhood growth and may help maintain bone density and prevent arterial stiffening in ageing. Silicon is found in wholegrains, oats, and some mineral waters.
Strontium(Sr)
Strontium is a trace mineral chemically similar to calcium that is incorporated into bone tissue and appears to promote bone growth and density. Recent research suggests that strontium works alongside calcium and vitamin D to support bone health, and some forms of strontium are used medically to treat severe osteoporosis. It is found naturally in seafood, wholegrains, and leafy vegetables.
Boron(B)
Boron is a trace mineral found in fruits, vegetables, and nuts that plays an important supporting role in bone health, brain function, and hormone balance. It helps the body use calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus more effectively, and may boost levels of testosterone and oestrogen. Boron deficiency impairs bone development and reduces vitamin C uptake.

Vitamin

10 parameters
Vitamin A
Vitamin A is essential for vision (especially in low light), healthy skin, and mucous membranes, and immune function. It is found in animal products as retinol (liver, dairy, eggs) and in colourful vegetables as beta-carotene (which the body converts to vitamin A). Deficiency causes night blindness, dry skin, and increased susceptibility to infections.
Vitamin B1
Vitamin B1 (thiamine) is needed to convert carbohydrates from food into energy. It is particularly important for the nervous system and heart. Deficiency — most common in people who drink heavily or eat mainly refined grains — causes nerve damage (beriberi), muscle weakness, numbness in the feet, and in severe cases heart and brain problems.
Vitamin B2
Vitamin B2 (riboflavin) helps the body break down fats, proteins, and carbohydrates, and supports the liver's detoxification processes. Deficiency causes cracks at the corners of the mouth, sore throat, inflamed tongue, and skin problems. Good sources include dairy products, eggs, meat, leafy vegetables, and fortified cereals.
Vitamin B3
Vitamin B3 (niacin) is essential for energy production, DNA repair, and skin health. It can be made in small amounts from the amino acid tryptophan. Niacin helps improve circulation, lower LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, and raise HDL cholesterol. Severe deficiency causes pellagra — a disease with symptoms of dermatitis, diarrhoea, and dementia.
Vitamin B6
Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) is essential for amino acid metabolism, making neurotransmitters (like serotonin and dopamine), and producing haemoglobin. It supports immune function and helps prevent arterial disease by reducing homocysteine levels. Deficiency causes anaemia, nerve damage, skin rashes, and a weakened immune system.
Vitamin B12
Vitamin B12 is essential for making red blood cells, maintaining the myelin sheath around nerves, and synthesising DNA. It is found almost exclusively in animal products. Deficiency — common in vegans, older adults, and those with poor gut absorption — causes pernicious anaemia, nerve damage, and cognitive decline. Symptoms can take years to appear.
Vitamin C (Ascorbic acid)
Vitamin C is a powerful antioxidant that the human body cannot make on its own. It is essential for building collagen (the structural protein of skin, blood vessels, tendons, and bone), absorbing iron, healing wounds, and fighting infections. Deficiency causes scurvy — with symptoms including easy bruising, bleeding gums, poor wound healing, and joint pain. Found in citrus fruits, berries, and peppers.
Vitamin D3
Vitamin D3 is the form of vitamin D made in your skin when exposed to sunlight. It is essential for absorbing calcium from the gut and depositing it in bones and teeth. Without adequate vitamin D, bones become soft and weak — causing rickets in children and osteomalacia (softening of bones) in adults. Most people in low-sun climates need a supplement, especially in winter.
Vitamin E
Vitamin E is a fat-soluble antioxidant that protects cell membranes from damage by free radicals. It is particularly important for protecting polyunsaturated fats in cell membranes from oxidation. Vitamin E also supports immune function and may slow skin ageing. Found in nuts, seeds, plant oils, and leafy greens. Severe deficiency is rare but causes nerve damage and muscle weakness.
Vitamin K
Vitamin K is essential for blood clotting (without it, wounds would not stop bleeding) and for directing calcium into bones rather than arteries. There are two main forms: K1 (from leafy greens, important for clotting) and K2 (from fermented foods and animal products, important for bone and cardiovascular health). Some medications (like warfarin) work by blocking vitamin K.

Amino Acid

11 parameters
Lysine
Lysine is an essential amino acid — your body cannot make it, so you must get it from food. It is critical for building proteins, making collagen and elastin (for skin and connective tissue), absorbing calcium, and producing carnitine (which converts fatty acids into energy). Lysine also supports the immune system and helps suppress the herpes virus. Good sources include meat, fish, dairy, and legumes.
Tryptophan
Tryptophan is an essential amino acid that the body uses to make serotonin (a neurotransmitter that regulates mood, sleep, and appetite) and melatonin (the sleep hormone). Adequate tryptophan promotes restful sleep, stable mood, and healthy appetite regulation. Low tryptophan is associated with depression, anxiety, and sleep problems. Found in turkey, eggs, cheese, nuts, and seeds.
Phenylalanine
Phenylalanine is an essential amino acid that the body converts into tyrosine, which is then used to make dopamine, adrenaline, noradrenaline, and thyroid hormones. These are critical for mood, alertness, and the stress response. Phenylalanine is found in protein-rich foods. People with the genetic condition PKU cannot process phenylalanine and must restrict it strictly.
Methionine
Methionine is an essential sulphur-containing amino acid that is important for detoxification, liver function, and making other sulphur-containing molecules including cysteine and the antioxidant glutathione. It supports the health of the liver, spleen, and lymph system. Deficiency causes poor growth, fatty liver, anaemia, and low energy. Found in meat, fish, eggs, and dairy.
Threonine
Threonine is an essential amino acid that helps form the connective proteins collagen and elastin in skin and joints. It also keeps moisture in the skin, promotes gut health by maintaining the intestinal lining, and supports the immune system. The body uses threonine to make glycine and serine, which are involved in liver function and muscle metabolism.
Isoleucine
Isoleucine is one of the three branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) that are particularly important for muscle metabolism and energy during exercise. It helps regulate blood sugar, promotes muscle repair, and plays a role in immune function. Low isoleucine causes muscle wasting, fatigue, and poor recovery from exercise. Found in meat, fish, eggs, dairy, and legumes.
Leucine
Leucine is the most potent BCAA for stimulating muscle protein synthesis — the process by which muscles rebuild after exercise. It also helps regulate blood sugar and provides energy to working muscles. Medical uses include treating sudden low blood sugar in children and as a nutritional supplement for muscle wasting conditions. Found abundantly in meat, dairy, eggs, and soy.
Valine
Valine is one of three branched-chain amino acids essential for muscle growth, tissue repair, and maintaining the nervous system. It is involved in regulating the corpus luteum and ovaries. Low valine causes neurological problems and muscle weakness. Like all BCAAs, valine is found primarily in meat, fish, dairy products, and eggs.
Histidine
Histidine is used by the body to make histamine (involved in immune responses and digestion), help absorb minerals (including iron), and protect nerves. It helps reduce gastric acidity, which is useful in managing peptic ulcers. Adequate histidine is important for preventing anaemia, maintaining nerve insulation (myelin sheath), and immune defence. Found in meat, poultry, fish, dairy, and whole grains.
Arginine
Arginine is used by the body to make nitric oxide, which relaxes and widens blood vessels, improving circulation. It also supports wound healing, hormone secretion, and the immune system. The liver uses arginine to convert toxic ammonia (a protein metabolism by-product) into urea for safe excretion. Arginine also helps with sperm production and male fertility.
Homocysteine
Homocysteine is a by-product of normal protein metabolism. While some is normal, elevated homocysteine levels are a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease (it damages blood vessel walls), stroke, kidney disease, and Alzheimer's disease. B vitamins — especially B6, B12, and folate — help keep homocysteine levels low by converting it back into useful amino acids.

Coenzyme

6 parameters
Nicotinamide
Nicotinamide (a form of vitamin B3) is a coenzyme involved in hundreds of metabolic reactions that produce cellular energy. It activates enzyme systems, supports the synthesis of DNA, proteins, and complex carbohydrates, and plays a role in regulating gene expression. It is also used to treat inflammatory skin conditions and is being studied for its role in anti-ageing.
Biotin
Biotin (vitamin B7) is a coenzyme essential for metabolising fats, proteins, and carbohydrates into energy. It is crucial for healthy hair, skin, and nails, and is often promoted for hair growth — though this mainly applies to those who are truly deficient. Biotin also supports normal nerve function and is found in eggs, liver, nuts, and seeds.
Pantothenic acid
Pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) is needed to convert food into energy and to metabolise fats. It is essential for the synthesis of coenzyme A, which powers cellular energy production. It also supports brain and nerve health, helps the adrenal glands produce stress hormones, and maintains healthy skin and hair. It is found in almost all foods.
Folic acid
Folic acid (folate, vitamin B9) is essential for making new DNA and cells, making it particularly important during periods of rapid growth — including pregnancy (where it prevents neural tube defects in developing babies). It also works with B12 and B6 to lower homocysteine levels, protecting the cardiovascular system. Deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia, fatigue, and in pregnancy, serious birth defects.
Coenzyme Q10
Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is a fat-soluble antioxidant found in virtually every cell of the body. It is essential for the cellular power stations (mitochondria) to produce energy. CoQ10 also protects cells from oxidative damage. Levels decline with age and are depleted by statin medications. Supplementation may benefit people with heart failure, migraines, and those on statins.
Glutathione
Glutathione is often called the body's 'master antioxidant'. Made from three amino acids, it is found in virtually every cell and protects cells from damage caused by free radicals, toxins, and oxidative stress. It also supports the immune system, detoxifies the liver, and helps recycle other antioxidants like vitamins C and E. Levels decline with age, chronic illness, and poor nutrition.

It is important for maintaining the skin's moisture barrier, supporting the cardiovascular system (it helps reduce LDL cholesterol), regulating inflammation, and supporting hormonal balance. Found in vegetable oils, nuts, and seeds. Most Western diets contain plenty.

Arachidonic acid (AA) is an omega-6 fatty acid that is a key structural component of brain cell membranes and plays a crucial role in brain development, particularly in infants. It is also a precursor to important signalling molecules that regulate inflammation, blood clotting, and immune responses. It is found in meat, eggs, and dairy products.

Endocrine System

7 parameters
Thyroid secretion index
The thyroid gland, located in the neck, produces hormones that act as the body's metabolic regulator — controlling how fast cells burn energy, heart rate, body temperature, and growth. This index measures how actively the thyroid is secreting hormones. An underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) causes fatigue, weight gain, and cold sensitivity. An overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) causes weight loss, heart palpitations, and anxiety.
Parathyroid hormone secretion index
The parathyroid glands (four tiny glands behind the thyroid) produce parathyroid hormone (PTH), which acts as the body's calcium manager. When blood calcium drops too low, PTH triggers bones to release calcium into the blood and tells the kidneys to retain more calcium. This index measures PTH secretion — abnormally high PTH can deplete bones of calcium and cause kidney stones.
Adrenal glands Index
The adrenal glands sit on top of the kidneys and produce adrenaline, noradrenaline, and cortisol. These are the hormones behind the 'fight or flight' stress response — they raise heart rate, blood pressure, and blood sugar to prepare the body for action. Chronically overactive adrenals (from prolonged stress) wear the body down; underactive adrenals (Addison's disease) cause fatigue, low blood pressure, and salt craving.
Pituitary secretion index
The pituitary gland is the 'master gland' — a pea-sized structure at the base of the brain that controls most other endocrine glands. It produces growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, ACTH (for the adrenals), and gonadotropins (for the sex organs). This index measures overall pituitary output. Pituitary problems can cascade into hormonal imbalances throughout the body.
Pineal secretion index
The pineal gland is a small gland in the brain that produces melatonin — the hormone that regulates your sleep-wake cycle (circadian rhythm). Melatonin production increases in darkness and decreases in light. This index measures pineal activity. Poor pineal function (common with ageing and excessive artificial light at night) causes sleep disorders, fatigue, and disrupted biological rhythms.
Thymus gland secretion index
The thymus is an immune gland located behind the breastbone. In childhood it is large and active, producing and maturing T-lymphocytes (T-cells) that are the cornerstone of the immune system's ability to fight infections and cancer. After puberty the thymus gradually shrinks. This index reflects how well the thymus is still functioning — important for immune resilience at any age.
Gland secretion index
This refers to the gonads — the testes in men and the ovaries in women. The testes produce testosterone, which drives male sexual development, sperm production, muscle mass, and bone density. The ovaries produce oestrogen and progesterone, which regulate the menstrual cycle, fertility, and maintain bone density and cardiovascular health. This index reflects the overall hormonal output of the reproductive glands.

Immune System

9 parameters
Lymph node Index
Lymph nodes are small, bean-shaped glands scattered throughout the body that act as filter stations in the lymphatic system. They trap bacteria, viruses, and other harmful particles and house immune cells that destroy them. When you have an infection, nearby lymph nodes swell as immune activity increases. This index reflects how well your lymph nodes are functioning as part of your immune defence.
Tonsil immune Index
The tonsils are clusters of lymphoid tissue at the back of the throat — they are your immune system's first responders to inhaled or swallowed pathogens. They contain T-cells, B-cells, and phagocytes (engulfing cells). This index reflects how well the tonsils are functioning as a frontline immune barrier in the throat.
Bone marrow Index
Bone marrow is the spongy tissue inside bones where all blood cells — including red blood cells (which carry oxygen), white blood cells (which fight infection), and platelets (which clot blood) — are produced. This index reflects how well the bone marrow is functioning. Poor marrow function can cause anaemia, infection susceptibility, and bleeding problems.
Spleen index
The spleen is the body's largest lymphoid organ, located in the upper left abdomen. It filters the blood, removing old and damaged red blood cells, and houses immune cells that detect and respond to blood-borne pathogens. The spleen also stores a reserve of blood and platelets. A damaged or removed spleen significantly increases infection risk, particularly from encapsulated bacteria.
Thymus index
The thymus is where T-lymphocytes (T-cells) mature into immune cells capable of distinguishing the body's own cells from foreign invaders. Active in childhood, the thymus shrinks after puberty but continues to produce a small number of new T-cells throughout life. This index reflects the thymus's ongoing contribution to immune surveillance and defence.
Immunoglobulin index
Immunoglobulins (antibodies) are specialised proteins produced by B-cells that recognise and neutralise specific pathogens (bacteria, viruses, toxins). They circulate in the blood and body fluids. This index measures the overall level and effectiveness of your antibody-based immune defence — high immunoglobulins indicate active immune defence; very low levels indicate immunodeficiency.
Respiratory immune Index
The respiratory tract — from the nose to the lungs — is constantly exposed to airborne pathogens. It has its own layered immune defence: mucus traps particles, tiny hairs (cilia) sweep them out, and lymphoid tissue in the airways produces antibodies and immune cells to neutralise threats. This index reflects how robust the respiratory immune defence system is.
Gastrointestinal immune Index
The gut immune system is the largest immune organ in the body — about 70% of immune cells reside in the digestive tract. The gut lining must absorb nutrients while blocking pathogens. Gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) produces antibodies (especially IgA) and regulates immune tolerance. This index measures how effectively your digestive tract is defending against harmful microbes and antigens.
Mucosa immune Index
The mucosal immune system protects all the body's mucous membrane surfaces — the gut, respiratory tract, urinary tract, and reproductive system. It is somewhat independent from the systemic immune system, producing a specialised antibody called secretory IgA (sIgA) that coats mucosal surfaces and prevents pathogens from attaching. This index reflects the strength of this critical first-contact immune barrier.

Thyroid

4 parameters
Free thyroxine (FT4)
Free thyroxine (FT4) is the active, unbound form of the main thyroid hormone (T4). Measuring FT4 gives the most accurate picture of thyroid gland function because it is not affected by changes in thyroid-binding proteins in the blood. High FT4 indicates an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism); low FT4 indicates an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism).
Thyroglobulin
Thyroglobulin is a large protein made in the thyroid gland that serves as the raw material for producing thyroid hormones (T3 and T4). It is stored in the thyroid follicles and released in small amounts into the bloodstream. Thyroglobulin is used as a tumour marker for monitoring thyroid cancer — elevated levels after thyroid cancer treatment can indicate cancer recurrence.
Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies
These are autoantibodies that the immune system mistakenly produces against thyroglobulin, the body's own thyroid protein. Their presence is a hallmark of autoimmune thyroid disease — particularly Hashimoto's thyroiditis (where the immune system gradually destroys the thyroid, causing hypothyroidism). Elevated levels help diagnose autoimmune thyroid conditions.
Three triiodothyronine (T3)
T3 (triiodothyronine) is the more biologically active thyroid hormone — it has about three to four times the potency of T4. Most T3 is made by converting T4 in tissues throughout the body. T3 regulates metabolism, heart rate, body temperature, and development. Low T3 causes metabolic slowdown; high T3 causes accelerated metabolism and heart palpitations.

Human Toxin

4 parameters
Stimulating Beverage
Stimulating beverages (such as energy drinks and high-caffeine drinks) lack the electrolytes and minerals the body loses through sweat. When consumed without proper hydration, they can lower the concentration of minerals in the blood, cause fluid imbalances, disrupt sleep, and stress the heart. Regular consumption is associated with increased blood pressure, anxiety, and — in excess — heart rhythm problems.
Electromagnetic Radiation
Electromagnetic radiation (EMR) is emitted by all electronic devices — mobile phones, WiFi routers, power lines, and microwave ovens. EMR spans a broad spectrum; the non-ionising forms (like WiFi and phone signals) are generally considered low-risk at normal exposure levels. However, prolonged heavy exposure — particularly to higher-frequency radiation — is being studied for potential effects on cell function and long-term health.
Free Radical
Free radicals are unstable molecules produced naturally during cell metabolism and also from external sources like pollution, UV radiation, cigarette smoke, and processed foods. They damage cells, proteins, and DNA — accelerating ageing and contributing to conditions like cancer, heart disease, and dementia. The body uses antioxidants (from food and produced internally) to neutralise free radicals.
Toxic Pesticide Residue
Pesticide residues are the traces of agricultural chemicals that remain on food, in water, and in the environment after application. While regulatory bodies set 'safe' maximum residue limits, concerns exist about the cumulative effects of low-level, long-term exposure from multiple pesticides. Possible effects include hormone disruption, nervous system effects, and increased cancer risk with chronic exposure.

Heavy Metal

8 parameters
Lead
Lead is a highly toxic heavy metal with no safe level of exposure. Even low blood lead levels cause neurological damage — particularly in children, where it impairs brain development, reduces IQ, and causes behaviour problems. In adults, lead exposure is associated with high blood pressure, kidney damage, and nerve damage. Sources include old paint, contaminated water pipes, and industrial dust.
Mercury
Mercury is a potent neurotoxin that accumulates in the brain, liver, and kidneys. Exposure can come from eating contaminated fish (especially large predatory fish like tuna and swordfish), dental amalgam fillings, and industrial pollution. Mercury poisoning damages the central nervous system, kidneys, and digestive system — causing tremors, memory loss, vision and hearing problems, and personality changes.
Cadmium
Cadmium is a toxic metal that accumulates in the body — primarily in the kidneys — over decades of low-level exposure. It is found in cigarette smoke, contaminated soil and food (especially leafy vegetables, grains, and shellfish), and industrial pollution. Long-term exposure damages the kidneys, weakens bones (causing the painful condition 'itai-itai disease'), and is classified as a human carcinogen.
Chromium
Chromium exists in two main forms. Trivalent chromium (Cr3+) is an essential trace mineral that helps insulin function and regulates blood sugar. Hexavalent chromium (Cr6+) — an industrial pollutant — is highly toxic, causing lung cancer with inhalation exposure, and damaging the stomach, liver, and kidneys with other routes of exposure. This scan likely measures toxic chromium accumulation.
Arsenic
Arsenic is a naturally occurring toxic metalloid that contaminates drinking water in some regions (notably Bangladesh and parts of South Asia and South America). Chronic arsenic exposure from contaminated water or food accumulates in hair, nails, bone, and organs. Long-term exposure causes skin lesions, peripheral nerve damage, liver damage, and significantly increases the risk of skin, bladder, and lung cancer.
Antimony
Antimony is a metalloid used in flame retardants, alloys, and some industrial processes. Exposure typically occurs through industrial work or contaminated environments. It irritates the eyes, nose, throat, and skin. Prolonged exposure can damage the heart and liver. High-level inhalation (antimony trioxide dust) causes respiratory disease and is classified as a possible carcinogen.
Thallium
Thallium is an extremely toxic heavy metal that was once used in rat poison and is now mainly an industrial by-product. Even small amounts are dangerous — it is a powerful nerve poison that causes rapid hair loss, peripheral nerve damage, and liver and kidney injury. Thallium can be absorbed through skin, inhaled, or ingested, making occupational exposure particularly hazardous.
Aluminum
Aluminium accumulates in the body over time, primarily in the brain, liver, and bones. While the evidence is not conclusive, elevated aluminium accumulation is associated with neurological effects — including memory loss, difficulty concentrating, and possibly contributing to the risk of dementia (aluminium is found in higher concentrations in the brains of Alzheimer's patients). Sources include cooking with aluminium cookware, certain antacids, deodorants, and processed foods.

Basic Physical Quality

5 parameters
Response Ability
This measures the speed and sharpness of your physical and mental reactions — how quickly and decisively you respond to stimulation. Good response ability reflects healthy adrenal gland function, strong willpower, and good mental resilience. Abnormally low scores suggest the adrenal glands may be under-secreting, causing sluggishness, low mood, and slow reactions.
Mental Power
This reflects the overall vitality and functional capacity of your brain — including thinking speed, alertness, and mental energy. A score in the normal range indicates healthy brain function. Below-normal scores may indicate weak brain function, depression, insomnia, or deteriorating thinking and memory.
Water Shortage
This measures the body's current hydration status. Normal values indicate adequate moisture in the body's tissues and cells. Below-normal values indicate the body is dehydrated — causing thirst, fatigue, reduced mental performance, and if sustained long-term, kidney stress and increased risk of urinary tract infections and kidney stones.
Hypoxia
This measures the oxygen level in the body's cells. Normal values mean cells are receiving adequate oxygen for metabolism. Abnormally low values suggest the respiratory system may not be functioning optimally, or the blood is not carrying oxygen efficiently (possibly anaemia), or there is insufficient physical activity to maintain good oxygenation.
PH
This measures the acid-alkaline balance of the blood. Normal blood pH is slightly alkaline (around 7.35–7.45). A value that is too alkaline can cause muscle cramps and pain. A value that is too acidic is associated with chronic disease, fatigue, and metabolic problems. The body normally regulates pH tightly through breathing and kidney function.

Allergy

13 parameters
Drug allergy index
Drug allergy is an abnormal immune response to a medication. Unlike a side effect (which can happen to anyone), an allergic reaction only occurs in people whose immune system has become sensitised to that drug. Reactions range from mild skin rashes and hives to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Common culprits include penicillin, aspirin, sulfonamides, and certain anaesthetics.
Alcohol allergy index
Alcohol allergy involves an immune response to alcohol or its breakdown products (particularly acetaldehyde). True alcohol allergy is rare and different from alcohol intolerance (where the body lacks the enzyme to process alcohol efficiently). Reactions include skin flushing, hives, runny nose, and in severe cases, anaphylaxis. Alcohol intolerance is more common and causes flushing, nausea, and rapid heartbeat.
Pollen allergy index
Pollen allergy (allergic rhinitis or hay fever) occurs when the immune system overreacts to pollen particles inhaled from the air. Symptoms include sneezing, runny nose, itchy and watery eyes, and nasal congestion. Pollen from grasses, trees, and weeds are the most common triggers. Symptoms are seasonal and most severe when pollen counts are highest.
Injection allergy index
Some people react allergically to injected medications or vaccines. The most well-known is penicillin allergy (affecting 5–6% of people). Allergic reactions to injections can range from localised swelling and hives to severe anaphylaxis. Penicillin, streptomycin, and biological serums are the most common culprits. Any route of administration and any dose can trigger a reaction in a sensitised individual.
Chemical products allergy index
Synthetic fibres and chemical products (such as those in cleaning products, cosmetics, and industrial chemicals) can trigger allergic skin reactions (contact dermatitis) in sensitive individuals. Chemical components in synthetic fabrics — derived from petroleum products — can cause itchy rashes, redness, and skin inflammation on contact.
Paint allergy index
Paint allergy is a form of contact dermatitis triggered by specific chemicals in paint — most commonly isocyanates, formaldehyde, and certain solvents. Reactions can include skin redness, itching, blistering, and respiratory irritation. Importantly, the allergy reflects the body's immune sensitivity to paint chemicals, not necessarily the quality of the paint.
Dust allergy index
Dust allergy is most often caused by house dust mites — microscopic creatures that live in bedding, carpets, and soft furnishings. Breathing in dust containing mite particles triggers immune reactions including a runny nose, itchy eyes, sneezing, and in susceptible people, asthma. Keeping the home well-ventilated and using allergen-proof covers helps manage symptoms.
Smoke allergy index
Smoke allergy means the immune system reacts to particles in smoke — from cigarettes, wood fires, or cooking smoke. Symptoms include sneezing, runny nose, and in some people, skin rashes and allergic dermatitis. Smoke also contains hundreds of chemical irritants that worsen asthma and other respiratory conditions.
Hair dye allergy index
Hair dye allergy is a form of allergic contact dermatitis caused by chemicals in hair colourants — most commonly paraphenylenediamine (PPD). Reactions typically appear 24–48 hours after dyeing and range from scalp itching and redness to severe swelling of the scalp, face, and neck. Repeated exposure increases sensitisation, making reactions progressively worse.
Animal fur allergy index
Animal fur allergy is a reaction to proteins found in animal dander (shed skin cells), saliva, or urine — not the fur itself. Cats and dogs are the most common triggers. Symptoms include runny nose, itchy eyes, skin rashes, and wheezing. The allergens are airborne and can persist in rooms even after the animal is removed.
Metal jewelry allergy index
Many people are allergic to metals — especially nickel, which is mixed into many cheaper metal alloys used in jewellery, belt buckles, and watch straps. When sweat and body heat dissolve tiny amounts of nickel from metal, it penetrates the skin and triggers an allergic reaction. Symptoms include redness, itching, blistering, and scaling at the contact point.
Seafood allergy index
Seafood allergies — to shellfish (shrimp, crab, lobster) or fish — are among the most common food allergies in adults. They are caused by immune reactions to specific proteins in seafood. Reactions can range from hives and swelling to vomiting, diarrhoea, and life-threatening anaphylaxis. Seafood allergies rarely resolve with age and are often lifelong.
Milk allergy index
Milk allergy is an immune reaction to proteins in cow's milk — primarily casein and whey. It is distinct from lactose intolerance (which is a digestive issue, not immune-based). Symptoms include eczema, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, and in severe cases, anaphylaxis. Common in infants and young children, though many outgrow it by school age.

Obesity

5 parameters
Abnormal lipid metabolism coefficient
Lipid metabolism refers to how the body processes, stores, and uses fats. Abnormal lipid metabolism — whether from genetics, hormonal imbalances, or diet — leads to unhealthy levels of fats in the blood (high triglycerides, high LDL, low HDL) and contributes to fat accumulation in the body and arteries. It is a key driver of obesity, heart disease, and fatty liver disease.
Brown adipose tissue abnormalities coefficient
Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is special fat that burns energy to produce heat — unlike white fat, which stores energy. BAT helps regulate body temperature and energy balance. In lean people, BAT is more active after eating or in cold environments (called thermogenesis). Abnormal or reduced BAT activity means less energy is burned as heat, contributing to weight gain and obesity.
Hyperinsulinemia coefficient
Hyperinsulinemia means chronically high insulin levels in the blood. This is strongly associated with obesity — excess body fat causes cells to become resistant to insulin, so the pancreas produces more to compensate. High insulin promotes further fat storage, creating a vicious cycle. Hyperinsulinemia is a key step in the progression from obesity to type 2 diabetes.
Nucleus of the hypothalamus abnormal coefficient
The hypothalamus in the brain acts as the body's appetite control centre, with specific nuclei that signal 'full' (satiety centre) or 'hungry' (hunger centre). Abnormalities in these control centres can cause the fullness signal to fail or the hunger signal to remain on — leading to chronic overeating, inability to feel full, and obesity that feels difficult to control despite effort.
Triglyceride content of abnormal coefficient
When you eat more calories than you burn, the excess energy is converted in the liver and muscles and stored as triglycerides in fat tissue. Chronically high triglycerides are an independent risk factor for heart disease and indicate that the body is accumulating excessive stored fat. High triglycerides are associated with obesity, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes.

Skin

11 parameters
Skin Free Radical Index
Free radicals are unstable molecules generated by UV radiation, pollution, cigarette smoke, and normal metabolism that damage skin cells, break down collagen, and accelerate ageing. This index measures the level of free radical damage in your skin. High free radical activity causes premature wrinkles, loss of elasticity, and dull, uneven skin tone. Antioxidants in skincare and diet counteract this.
Skin Collagen Index
Collagen is the main structural protein of the skin — it provides firmness and elasticity. Skin collagen levels decline naturally from the mid-20s onward, and faster with UV exposure, smoking, high sugar intake, and stress. This index measures the density and quality of collagen in your skin. Low collagen leads to sagging, wrinkles, and thinning skin.
Skin Grease Index
This measures the level of oil (sebum) produced by your skin's sebaceous glands. Oily skin (high sebum) has a shiny appearance, large pores, and is prone to acne and blackheads. Dry skin (low sebum) feels tight, flaky, and is prone to irritation. Combination skin has oily areas (typically the T-zone) and dry areas. The right balance keeps skin moisturised without clogging pores.
Oily skin
Oily skin occurs when sebaceous glands overproduce sebum. The skin looks shiny, feels greasy, and has enlarged pores. While oily skin ages better than dry skin (it is less prone to wrinkles), it is more prone to acne, blackheads, and whiteheads. Care involves gentle oil-free cleansing, non-comedogenic moisturisers, and regular exfoliation — avoiding harsh stripping, which makes oiliness worse.
Skin Immunity Index
The skin is the body's largest immune organ — it contains specialised immune cells (Langerhans cells, T-cells, mast cells) that detect and respond to pathogens, allergens, and UV damage. This index measures how robust this skin immune defence is. Strong skin immunity prevents infections, controls inflammatory conditions like eczema and psoriasis, and reduces skin cancer risk.
Skin Moisture Index
Skin needs adequate moisture in its outermost layer (the stratum corneum) to remain smooth, supple, and elastic. Dry skin lacks this moisture barrier. This index measures the moisture content of the skin's surface. Low values indicate dry, dehydrated skin that is prone to cracking, irritation, and premature ageing.
Skin Moisture Loss
Transepidermal water loss (TEWL) measures how much water is evaporating from the skin's surface. A healthy skin barrier keeps moisture locked in. When the barrier is damaged — by harsh soaps, cold weather, ageing, or skin conditions — water evaporates rapidly, leaving skin dry, tight, and irritated. Moisturisers and barrier creams help reduce moisture loss.
Skin Red Blood Trace Index
Red blood traces (telangiectasia) are tiny dilated capillaries visible just under the skin — appearing as red lines or patches on the face, chest, or legs. They are caused by chronic UV exposure, rosacea, excessive heat exposure, pregnancy hormones, or ageing of the capillary walls. While generally harmless, prominent telangiectasia can be treated with laser therapy.
Skin Elasticity Index
Skin elasticity is the ability of the skin to stretch and then spring back to its original shape. It depends on the health of collagen and elastin fibres in the dermis. UV radiation, smoking, ageing, and dehydration all break down these fibres, causing the skin to sag and wrinkle. Good nutrition (especially antioxidants and protein), sun protection, and adequate hydration support skin elasticity.
Skin Melanin Index
Melanin is the pigment that gives skin, hair, and eyes their colour and absorbs UV radiation to protect DNA from sun damage. Melanin is produced by melanocytes in the lower layers of the epidermis. This index measures melanin levels in the skin. Uneven melanin distribution causes hyperpigmentation (dark spots), while loss of melanin produces light patches (as in vitiligo).
Skin Horniness Index
The stratum corneum (skin corneum layer) is the outermost layer of skin — made of dead, flattened cells filled with keratin that form a tough, waterproof barrier. Normal skin constantly sheds these cells and replaces them. This index measures the thickness and health of this outermost barrier layer. Too thick (hyperkeratosis) causes rough, dull skin; too thin means a compromised barrier that loses moisture easily.

Eye

8 parameters
Bags under the eyes
Under-eye bags are caused by weakening of the tissues and muscles around the eyes that normally hold the fat pads in place. As the orbital septum (a membrane that keeps fat contained) loses strength — due to ageing, genetics, fluid retention, or poor sleep — the fat herniates forward, creating a puffy, pouchy appearance under the eyes.
Collagen eye wrinkle
Eye wrinkles (crow's feet and under-eye lines) form when the collagen and elastic fibres in the delicate skin around the eyes break down. The skin around the eyes is the thinnest on the body, making it particularly vulnerable. Collagen gives skin its firmness; elastin allows it to spring back. When these break down through ageing, UV exposure, and repeated facial expressions, permanent lines form.
Dark circles
Dark circles under the eyes are caused by several factors: transparent skin revealing dark blood vessels beneath, venous congestion causing blood to pool and become deoxygenated (turning bluish), hyperpigmentation from sun exposure or inflammation, loss of volume causing shadows, or a combination of these. Poor sleep, dehydration, allergies, and ageing all contribute.
Lymphatic obstruction
The lymphatic system drains excess fluid, waste products, and immune cells from tissues. Lymphatic obstruction — from inflammation, cancer, injury, or surgery — causes fluid to accumulate in surrounding tissues (lymphoedema). Around the eyes, poor lymphatic drainage contributes to morning puffiness. The condition can be primary (no known cause) or secondary (from infection, cancer, or radiation).
Sagging
Skin around the eyes sags when the structural supports weaken — collagen and elastin fibres break down, subcutaneous fat diminishes, and the underlying muscles relax. Gravity also plays a role over decades of downward pull. The result is drooping upper eyelids, lower eyelid bags, and loss of the crisp eye contour of youth.
Edema
Eye oedema (swelling) occurs when excess fluid accumulates in the tissues around the eyes. This can result from poor blood and lymphatic circulation that cannot efficiently remove waste fluid, causing it to pool in the capillaries and leak into surrounding tissue. Causes include poor sleep, high salt intake, alcohol, allergies, kidney problems, and thyroid disease.
Eye cell activity
This reflects the vitality and metabolic activity of the cells in and around the eye — including retinal cells, lens cells, and the cells of the cornea and conjunctiva. Good cell activity means these tissues are maintaining themselves, repairing damage, and functioning well. Reduced cell activity is associated with degenerative eye conditions, including macular degeneration and cataracts.
Visual fatigue
Visual fatigue (eye strain or asthenopia) occurs when the eyes are overused — particularly during prolonged close work, screen use, or reading in poor light. Symptoms include tired, sore, or burning eyes, blurred or double vision, headaches, and difficulty focusing. People with uncorrected refractive errors (short sight, long sight, astigmatism) are particularly susceptible.

Collagen

15 parameters
Eye
When the body is deficient in collagen, the delicate eye structures are affected — causing dry eyes, eye fatigue, and spontaneous tearing. The cornea (the clear front surface of the eye) may lose transparency, and the lens can become cloudy, potentially contributing to cataract formation. Collagen is a key structural component of the cornea and sclera (white of the eye).
Tooth
Collagen forms the organic matrix of dentine (the material beneath tooth enamel) and supports the gums and periodontal ligaments that anchor teeth. Collagen loss leads to calcium loss from teeth, increased susceptibility to decay and gum disease, tooth loosening, and sensitivity. Healthy collagen levels support strong, well-anchored teeth.
Hair and skin
Collagen provides the structural scaffolding for both skin and hair follicles. When collagen declines, hair becomes dry and brittle, hair loss increases, dandruff worsens, and hair may split or break more easily. In the skin, reduced collagen causes sagging (particularly cheeks, chin, and eye area), increased wrinkles, and loss of definition along the jawline and face contour.
Endocrine system
Collagen loss affects connective tissue throughout the endocrine system. In women, this can contribute to hormonal disruptions — including irregular or absent periods, early menopause, breast changes, and decreased pelvic support. In men, collagen depletion may contribute to erectile dysfunction and reduced testosterone production. The structural integrity of glandular tissues depends on collagen.
Circulatory system
Collagen makes up the walls of blood vessels, giving them strength and flexibility. When collagen declines, blood vessel walls become less elastic and more fragile — contributing to blood pressure instability, increased risk of fatty liver and high cholesterol, slower circulation, and greater vulnerability to cardiovascular disease.
Digestive system
Collagen forms the connective tissue that supports abdominal organs. Loss of collagen leads to weakening of the abdominal wall, contributing to organ prolapse (organs dropping from their normal position), digestive problems, and loss of abdominal muscle tone. Liver detoxification and secretory functions may also be impaired.
Immune system
Collagen supports the structural framework of lymphatic vessels and immune organs. When lymphatic vessel walls weaken, lymph circulation slows, reducing immune surveillance and response. This leads to decreased overall immunity, increased susceptibility to infections, muscle pain, and generalised physical weakness.
Motion systems
Collagen is the primary structural protein of cartilage, ligaments, and tendons — the connective tissues that hold joints together and allow smooth movement. Collagen depletion leads to joint pain, reduced flexibility, increased susceptibility to rheumatic conditions, bone spur formation, and in the spine, blocked energy flow and poor metabolism of the back muscles.
Muscle Tissue
Collagen forms the connective tissue sheath (fascia) around and between muscle fibres. When this supporting tissue weakens, muscles lose tone, lactic acid accumulates more easily (causing post-exercise soreness), and fat may replace muscle tissue. The result is muscle fatigue, cervical muscle stiffness (contributing to cervical spondylosis), and reduced physical performance.
Fat Metabolism
Collagen is involved in the structural integrity of fat cell (adipocyte) compartments. When collagen declines, fat metabolism becomes less efficient, fat accumulates more readily, and the body tends towards a more acidic state. This contributes to fatigue, digestive discomfort, and increased risk of metabolic conditions including diabetes, high blood pressure, and liver and kidney problems.

Detoxification and metabolism
Collagen supports the liver's structural integrity, which is essential for efficient detoxification. When liver collagen declines, toxin clearance slows — causing yellowing skin, rough skin texture, constipation, and general toxin accumulation in the body. The kidneys and spleen are also affected, increasing the risk of nephritis and progressive organ dysfunction.
Reproductive system
Pelvic floor tissues rely heavily on collagen for strength and support. Collagen loss leads to weakening of these tissues, potentially causing uterine prolapse, urinary incontinence, and ovarian changes. In women, vaginal tissue changes and menstrual irregularities may occur. In men, reduced pelvic support may contribute to erectile difficulties.
Nervous system
Collagen-derived amino acids are precursors for neurotransmitters that regulate brain function. Collagen loss can therefore contribute to memory problems, difficulty concentrating, insomnia, and anxiety. Additionally, collagen supports the myelin sheaths that protect nerves, and depletion may impair nerve signal transmission.
Skeleton
About 80% of the organic (non-mineral) component of bone is collagen. This collagen scaffolding gives bone its flexibility and tensile strength — without it, bone becomes brittle even if calcium levels are adequate. Collagen loss leads to reduced bone density, hollow bone structure, and greatly increased fracture risk. It is a major underlying factor in osteoporosis.

Pulse of heart and brain

5 parameters
Stroke Index
The amount of blood your heart pumps out with each beat, adjusted for your body size. It gives a personalised measure of how efficiently your heart is working. A low stroke index may mean your heart is not pumping strongly enough, causing fatigue and poor circulation.
Stroke Volume (SV)
The amount of blood (in millilitres) your left ventricle pumps out per heartbeat. A healthy adult pumps about 60–100 mL per beat. Low stroke volume may indicate a weak heart, blood loss, or poor fluid levels.
Heart Peripheral Resistance (TRR)
The resistance your heart faces when pushing blood through your blood vessels. High resistance means your vessels are narrowed or stiff, forcing your heart to work harder — raising blood pressure and increasing heart disease risk.
Pulse Wave Coefficient K
Measures how quickly and forcefully the pressure wave from each heartbeat travels through your arteries. Stiffer arteries (from aging or disease) increase this value and are a risk factor for heart attack and stroke.
Cerebrovascular Blood Oxygen Saturation (Sa)
How well oxygenated the blood is in your brain's blood vessels. The brain is extremely sensitive to oxygen levels — even brief reductions can cause confusion, memory problems, or stroke. Normal saturation ensures your brain cells receive enough fuel.

Blood lipids

6 parameters
Blood Viscosity
How thick and sticky your blood is. Thicker blood flows more slowly and puts extra strain on the heart. High viscosity increases the risk of blood clots, stroke, and heart attacks. Staying hydrated and exercising regularly helps keep blood viscosity healthy.
Total Cholesterol (TC)
All types of cholesterol combined. High total cholesterol increases the risk of fatty deposits building up in your arteries, which can lead to heart attack or stroke.
Triglyceride (TG)
The most common type of fat stored in your body, used for energy. High triglyceride levels are linked to overeating, excessive sugar or alcohol intake, and inactivity. They raise the risk of heart disease and can indicate prediabetes.
High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL-C)
The "good cholesterol." HDL carries cholesterol away from your arteries back to the liver for removal. Higher HDL levels reduce your risk of heart disease. Exercise and healthy fats help raise HDL.
Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL-C)
The "bad cholesterol." LDL carries cholesterol to your arteries where it can build up as plaque. High LDL is a major risk factor for heart attacks and strokes. Diet, exercise, and sometimes medication are used to lower it.
Neutral Fat (MB)
The primary form of fat storage in the body (mainly triglycerides). High levels combined with low HDL significantly increase cardiovascular risk and are associated with metabolic syndrome.

Prostate

3 parameters
Degree of Prostatic Hyperplasia
Prostatic hyperplasia (benign prostatic enlargement, BPH) is the non-cancerous enlargement of the prostate gland that affects most men from their 40s onward. As the prostate grows, it squeezes the urethra (the tube that carries urine), causing urinary symptoms: needing to urinate urgently and frequently, weak urine flow, difficulty starting or stopping urination, and incomplete bladder emptying.
Degree of Prostatic Calcification
Prostatic calcification refers to calcium deposits that form in prostate tissue — usually as scarring left behind after repeated bouts of prostatitis (prostate inflammation). These deposits are often visible on ultrasound. Significant calcification is associated with chronic prostatitis and may increase susceptibility to ongoing inflammation.
Prostatitis Syndrome
Prostatitis syndrome refers to inflammation of the prostate gland — a condition affecting about 25–30% of men attending urology clinics. It can range from completely symptom-free to causing significant pelvic pain, urinary difficulties, painful ejaculation, and general discomfort. It can be caused by bacterial infection or by non-infectious inflammation, and chronic cases can be frustrating and difficult to treat.

Male Sexual Function

3 parameters
Testosterone
Testosterone is the primary male sex hormone, produced mainly in the testes. It is responsible for male sexual development during puberty (voice deepening, genital development, body and facial hair), maintaining sex drive, sperm production, muscle mass, bone density, and red blood cell production. Testosterone levels peak in early adulthood and gradually decline from around age 30. Low testosterone causes fatigue, reduced libido, erectile difficulties, and muscle loss.
Gonadotropin
Gonadotropins are hormones produced by the pituitary gland that control the function of the testes and ovaries. In men, LH stimulates testosterone production and FSH stimulates sperm production. Insufficient gonadotropin secretion leads to underdeveloped reproductive organs, delayed puberty, low testosterone, and infertility. Excess gonadotropin early in life can trigger premature puberty.
Erection Transmitter
Erection is triggered by the release of chemical neurotransmitters (primarily nitric oxide) from nerve endings in the penis. These chemicals relax the smooth muscle of penile blood vessels, allowing about 200 mL of blood to rush in and compress the veins — trapping blood in the penile tissue and creating an erection. Any disruption to this signalling — from nerve damage, vascular disease, or hormonal imbalance — causes erectile dysfunction.

Sperm and semen

4 parameters
Semen Volume
The total amount of fluid produced during ejaculation. Normal volume is 1.5–5 mL. Very low volume may indicate blockage or hormonal issues. Very high volume can dilute sperm concentration. Both extremes can affect fertility.
Liquefying Time
Semen normally liquefies (becomes fluid) within 15–30 minutes after ejaculation, allowing sperm to swim freely. If it takes longer than 60 minutes, sperm can be trapped and unable to reach an egg.
Number of Sperms
The total number of sperm cells in a semen sample. A healthy count is at least 15 million per mL (39 million total). Low sperm count is a common cause of male infertility and can result from hormonal imbalances, infections, or lifestyle factors.
Sperm Motility Rate
The percentage of sperm that can move properly. At least 40% of sperm should be moving, with 32% moving in a forward direction. Poor motility is a major cause of male infertility.

Male Hormone

6 parameters
Androgen
Androgens (primarily testosterone) are the male sex hormones responsible for developing and maintaining male sexual characteristics: body and facial hair, deep voice, muscle mass, bone density, and sperm production. They also maintain libido and reproductive function. Androgens are produced mainly by the testes, with small amounts from the adrenal glands. Low androgen levels cause fatigue, reduced muscle mass, low libido, and mood changes.
Estrogen
Men also produce small amounts of oestrogen — mainly by converting testosterone in fat cells, skin, and other tissues (a process called aromatisation). Oestrogen in men helps regulate sex drive, erectile function, sperm production, and bone density. The right balance is important — too much oestrogen (or too little testosterone relative to oestrogen) can cause gynecomastia (breast tissue enlargement), reduced libido, and fertility problems.
Progesterone(P)
Men produce small amounts of progesterone, primarily in the adrenal glands and testes. In normal quantities, it balances testosterone and supports libido, mood, and sleep. When male progesterone is too high (relative to testosterone), it can suppress the effects of testosterone — potentially causing reduced sexual interest, fatigue, and other symptoms of testosterone deficiency.
Luteinizing Hormone (LH)
In men, LH (secreted by the pituitary gland) signals the Leydig cells in the testes to produce testosterone. LH is part of a feedback loop: low testosterone causes the pituitary to release more LH; high testosterone suppresses it. Measuring LH helps distinguish whether low testosterone is caused by testicular failure (high LH, low testosterone) or pituitary/brain problems (low LH and low testosterone).
Prolactin(PRL)
Prolactin is a hormone produced by the pituitary gland — known mainly for stimulating breast milk production in women. In men, elevated prolactin suppresses testosterone production and can cause low libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility, and gynecomastia (breast enlargement). High prolactin in men is usually caused by a benign pituitary tumour (prolactinoma), certain medications, or chronic stress.
Follicle Stimulating Hormone(FSH)
In men, FSH (secreted by the pituitary gland) stimulates the Sertoli cells in the testes to support sperm production and maturation. Measuring FSH helps assess fertility — very high FSH in a man with low sperm count suggests the testes are not responding to stimulation (primary testicular failure). Low FSH with low sperm count suggests a pituitary or hormonal problem.

Human Immunity

5 parameters
Tissue Barrier Immune Index
Your body's first line of defence — skin, mucous membranes, and the linings of your gut and lungs. These barriers physically block pathogens from entering. This index measures how intact and effective these protective surfaces are.
Innate Immune Cell Index
The rapid-response soldiers of your immune system — natural killer cells, macrophages, and neutrophils. They respond within minutes to hours of detecting an infection, without needing prior exposure to the pathogen.
Innate Immune Molecule Index
Chemical molecules such as interferons, complement proteins, and cytokines that fight infection by alerting other immune cells, directly killing pathogens, or creating inflammation to isolate an infection.
Cellular Immune Index
T-lymphocytes (T-cells) that directly attack infected or cancerous cells. This branch of immunity is essential for controlling viral infections and preventing cancer. Low cellular immunity leaves the body vulnerable to chronic infections.
Humoral Immunity Index
B-lymphocytes (B-cells) that produce antibodies — proteins that recognise and neutralise specific pathogens. This index reflects your body's ability to produce targeted antibodies against bacteria, viruses, and other threats.

Respiratory Function

4 parameters
Volume Tidal(VT)
Tidal volume is the amount of air inhaled and exhaled with each normal, relaxed breath — typically about 500 mL in a healthy adult at rest. It naturally adjusts based on body size, metabolic demands, and activity level. During exercise, tidal volume increases to deliver more oxygen. Consistently low tidal volume at rest may indicate restrictive lung disease or respiratory muscle weakness.
Inspiratory Volume(TI)
Inspiratory capacity is the maximum amount of air you can inhale starting from a normal relaxed breath out — about 3,000 mL in a healthy adult. It reflects how much extra breath reserve you have above your resting tidal volume. A reduced inspiratory volume can indicate lung disease, weakened breathing muscles, or restricted chest expansion.
Functional Residual Capacity(FRC)
Functional residual capacity is the amount of air left in the lungs after a normal relaxed breath out — typically about 2,300–2,500 mL. This residual air keeps the airways open and allows gas exchange to continue between breaths. In emphysema, FRC increases as air becomes trapped in over-inflated lungs. In fibrosis, FRC decreases as stiff lungs deflate further than normal.
Residual Volume(RV)
Residual volume is the air that remains in the lungs even after you have exhaled as forcefully as possible — it cannot be breathed out. Typically about 1,200 mL, it prevents the lungs from collapsing completely. In emphysema, residual volume increases significantly as damaged airways trap air. Residual volume is measured indirectly (since it cannot be breathed into a spirometer) using gas dilution or body plethysmography.

Lecithin

6 parameters
Phospholipid index
Phospholipids are fat molecules that form the double-layered membrane (the bilayer) surrounding every cell in the body. This membrane controls what enters and exits the cell. Phospholipids also help transport fats through the bloodstream, prevent fatty liver, lower blood cholesterol, and improve circulation. Adequate phospholipids are essential for cell membrane health throughout the body.
Sphingolipid index
Sphingolipids are complex lipids that are key structural components of cell membranes — particularly in nerve cells and brain tissue. They are especially concentrated in the central nervous system, where they help form the myelin sheaths that insulate nerve fibres and speed up nerve signal transmission. Abnormal accumulation of sphingolipids in tissues causes serious neurological diseases (sphingolipidoses).
Sphingomyelin index
Sphingomyelin is a type of sphingolipid that is a major building block of animal cell membranes. It plays a crucial role in maintaining cell membrane integrity and is particularly important in the nervous system, where it forms part of the myelin sheath around nerve fibres. Sphingomyelin levels affect cell signalling and are being studied in relation to cardiovascular disease and Alzheimer's disease.
Lecithin index
Lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) is a phospholipid that is sometimes called the 'third essential nutrient' alongside proteins and vitamins. It is a major component of cell membranes and is essential for brain function (as a precursor to the neurotransmitter acetylcholine), liver health (preventing fat accumulation), and fat metabolism. Good dietary sources include egg yolks, sunflower seeds, and soybeans.
Brain phospholipid index
Brain phospholipids are natural, biologically active fats concentrated in brain cell membranes. They support the brain's unique functions — including cell-to-cell signalling, neurotransmitter release, and maintaining the fluidity and integrity of brain cell membranes. Adequate brain phospholipids are associated with sharper cognitive function and better mood regulation. They are non-toxic, non-irritating, and naturally derived.
Liposomal index
Liposomes are microscopic spheres made from phospholipids (primarily lecithin) that have the same double-layer structure as cell membranes. In the body's own tissues, this type of structure is used to protect and transport lipid-soluble molecules. In skincare, liposomal formulations can penetrate deeply into skin tissue, delivering active ingredients directly into skin cells and providing excellent moisture retention.

Fatty acid

4 parameters
Saturated fatty acid index
Saturated fatty acids are fats where all carbon atoms are fully bonded to hydrogen atoms — making them solid at room temperature (like butter and lard). They are found mainly in animal products and tropical oils. A high saturated fat intake raises LDL (bad) cholesterol and is associated with increased cardiovascular disease risk. However, not all saturated fats are equally harmful — short and medium-chain ones behave differently from long-chain saturated fats.
Unsaturated fatty acid index
Unsaturated fatty acids — both monounsaturated (olive oil, avocado) and polyunsaturated (fish oil, flaxseed) — are liquid at room temperature and are associated with health benefits. They help lower LDL cholesterol, reduce inflammation, support brain function, and maintain healthy cell membranes. Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated fats is consistently linked to reduced cardiovascular risk.
Essential fatty acid index
Essential fatty acids (omega-3 and omega-6) cannot be made by the body and must come from food. They are critical for brain development, immune function, hormone production, and controlling inflammation. Omega-3s (from oily fish, flaxseeds, walnuts) help reduce inflammation; omega-6s (from vegetable oils) promote it in excess. The balance between these two is important for overall health.
Triglyceride index
Triglycerides are the most common fat molecules in the body — formed when glycerol combines with three fatty acids. They are the primary form of stored energy and circulate in the bloodstream. Elevated blood triglycerides are an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease and are closely associated with other metabolic risk factors, including low HDL cholesterol, high blood sugar, and abdominal obesity.
